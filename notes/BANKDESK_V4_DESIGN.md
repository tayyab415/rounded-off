# BankDesk v4: an agent that investigates

2026-11-16 00:20 IST. Design only; nothing built. Replaces section 6 of `notes/BANKDESK_PROPOSAL.md`.
Tayyab's criticism: the agent hears "WhatsApp" and blocks. A classifier could do that. A person in that seat would
look at the customer, the payee and the story, then ask only what is still unknown.

## 1. What the agent does today (measured, GRPO3 bf16, 444 test episodes)

- **It runs a script.** Five tool sequences cover 375 of 444 episodes (84%). The longest has one question in it.
- **It never asks more than twice**, and it questions 72% of genuine customers on ambiguous requests (98% of scams).
- **The answers give the label away.** A keyword rule written in one pass (WhatsApp, unknown, officer, recruiter, ...)
  on the two answers the agent usually asks for catches 94% of scams on the English test cards and flags 19% of
  genuine ones. "Told to keep it secret: yes" appears in 28% of scam cards and no genuine card. The rule is crude and
  untuned; the point is that the cards let it work.
- **A cover story beats it.** Coached victims get paid out in 52% (EN) and 57% (HI) of ambiguous scams.

The environment made this. Six fixed facts per customer, no history, free questions, honest victims, a teacher told
which two questions to ask, and a 50/50 scam mix where blocking on any cue is cheap.

## 2. What makes it an investigation and not a classifier

Four rules for the environment. Each one can be tested.

1. **No single source settles a case.** Every source is wrong at a known rate. The truth shows only when sources are
   combined, most often as a contradiction between two of them.
2. **Evidence has different prices.** A look-up costs seconds. A question costs the customer's patience. A hold on a
   genuine payment costs the most.
3. **Stakes set the effort.** ₹2,000 to a known payee and a ₹4.8 lakh FD break to a new one do not get the same care.
4. **The decision is graded and comes with reasons.** Pay, delay, call back, hold, refuse; with a note that cites
   what was found.

## 3. The world behind each card

Each card becomes a small world, generated by code from a spec, so every fact is checkable.

| Layer | What it holds | Scam fingerprints that sit there |
|---|---|---|
| Customer | age, years with the bank, usual payees and amounts, salary day, balance, FDs | task scams send small bait credits from strangers for days before the ask; limit raised yesterday; FD broken this morning |
| Payee | account age, personal or business, registered name, distinct senders today, money out within minutes, reports by other customers, paid before by this customer | many strangers paying in and fast withdrawal (mule); fresh personal account claimed as a company |
| Named things | company (registry entry, age), phone number (complaint count), link or app (domain age), a small set of web results | company does not exist; or it exists and the caller is not from it |
| Session | call in progress, saved contact or not, call length, screen share, new device, new SIM | 47-minute call with an unsaved number while banking |
| Past cases | earlier holds, warnings and notes on this customer | held yesterday, back today with a new payee or a split amount |

**Contradictions are the evidence.** Examples the cards are built around:

- "It's my company's fee" and the payee is a three-day-old personal savings account.
- "My landlord" and the customer has paid a different landlord account for 14 months.
- "Amazon HR" is a real company; the number is not Amazon's and the domain is nine days old. A web search that
  only confirms "Amazon exists" misleads.
- "Nobody asked me to" and the session shows a long live call with an unsaved number.
- A payee that looks like a mule (new, busy) and is a genuine wedding caterer in season.

**Minimal pairs.** For every cue there is a genuine card and a scam card that share it: a real contractor found on
WhatsApp and a WhatsApp task scam; a flagged payee that is honest and a clean one that is a mule. A cue-reader
fails these by construction.

## 4. Fetch first, then one form for the gaps (Tayyab, 16 Nov)

A bank already holds most of what the agent needs. So the agent looks things up before it asks anything, and what
it still needs it asks in one short form built for this case. Free chat questions become the exception.

**The case file.** Every request type has a playbook: the facts needed to decide it. For "pay a new payee" that
is who the payee is, how the customer knows them, what the payment is for, how the customer got the account
details, who started the request, and why now. Each fact in the file has a status:

| Status | Meaning |
|---|---|
| on record | the bank's own data answers it |
| claimed | the customer said it; nothing confirms it |
| checked | the customer's answer matches a record or registry |
| contradicted | the customer's answer disagrees with a record |
| missing | nobody has answered it yet |

**The flow.**
1. `playbook(request_type)` lists the facts this kind of request needs.
2. Silent look-ups fill in what the bank knows.
3. If nothing that matters is missing or contradicted, decide. No form.
4. Otherwise `request_details(fields)`: one form, five fields at most, only for facts still missing or
   contradicted.
5. The form comes back with each answer already checked against records and registries.
6. Decide. The case note is the case file.

**Field types.**
- pick from options the bank pulled from its records ("which of these five payments?");
- yes or no;
- a reference that can be checked: invoice number, GSTIN, policy number, booking reference;
- a document;
- confirm a fact: the form shows what the bank found and asks the customer to explain it.

Never a field for an OTP, PIN, password, CVV or full card number. That is an automatic fail.

**One opening, three customers.** "Send ₹18,500 to Priya Sharma, urgent work payment."

| Customer | What the records say | What the agent does |
|---|---|---|
| A | paid this account six times in eight months; account four years old | no form; pays |
| B | never paid her; account three days old, personal, 41 senders today; small credits from five strangers this week; on a 38-minute call with an unsaved number | form below |
| C | never paid her; account is a two-year-old business account, busy every weekend | form: invoice number and GSTIN; registry name matches the account; pays |

Form for B:
1. What is this payment for? (goods or services I received / a fee or deposit to get a job, loan, prize or refund /
   someone I know personally / other)
2. Name and website of the company that asked for it.
3. You received small credits from five new senders this week. Are they connected to this payment? (yes / no)
4. Is someone on a call with you now, guiding you? (yes / no)

B's company is not in the registry, and B answers "no" to field 4 while the session shows a call. Two facts are
contradicted. The agent holds, and its message names both.

**Why a form beats chat questions.**
- A fixed list of questions can be rehearsed with the scammer (research note 03). A form built from this
  customer's own records cannot be prepared for.
- Answers are structured, so code can check them against records and code can score the form.
- One round with the customer, mostly taps, in place of several typed replies.
- It works for every request, which gives the wider agent: a dispute form lists the customer's recent payments
  to pick from; a lost-card form lists their cards.
- For a 3B model a form is one structured action, not a long conversation to keep track of.

**Scoring a form in code.** Each world lists the facts needed, the ones on record and the ones that decide the
case. Every field names a fact from the playbook's catalogue (about 40 fact types, or `other`); the label is free
text in the customer's language.

## 4b. Tools: 14, shown seven at a time

| Stage | Tool | Returns |
|---|---|---|
| Case | `playbook(request_type)` | the facts this request needs |
| Case | `customer_profile()` | age band, tenure, typical monthly outflow, recent changes, past cases |
| Case | `history(payee or days)` | a summary, not a dump: "paid 14 times, about ₹12,000, between the 1st and 5th" |
| Case | `payee_check(account)` | age, type, name, senders today, time to withdrawal, reports |
| Case | `session_check()` | call, contact saved or not, minutes, screen share, device, SIM |
| Case | `entity_check(name, phone or link)` | registry entry, complaint count, domain age, three web result lines |
| Case | `risk_scores()` | three classifier scores, below |
| Customer | `request_details(fields)` | the filled form, each answer marked checked, claimed or contradicted |
| Customer | `ask_customer(question)` | one free-text follow-up, for what a form cannot capture |
| Customer | `callback()` | calls the registered number; a customer on a live scam call cannot take it |
| Act | `proceed()` | prepares the requested payment; the customer approves in their app |
| Act | `delay(hours, reason)` | cancellable hold |
| Act | `hold_and_escalate()` | stops, hands the case file to the fraud team |
| Act | `final_reply(message)` | answer, warning or refusal |

**Classifiers as tools.** The small model reads scores; it does not have to be the classifier.

- Mule score for the payee account, a small model fitted on the train worlds.
- Anomaly score for this payment against the customer's own history, plain statistics.
- Scam-type score for the story so far.

Each score is wrong on purpose at a set rate, and some cards are built where a score is confidently wrong. If a
score were enough, the agent would turn back into a threshold. Rule check for Tayyab: the first two are ordinary
code. The third is a neural model, and the track says only Cohere may be trained, so it should be a small head on
Tiny Aya itself or be dropped.

## 5. The customer

- **A timeline, not six slots.** About 30 facts: who contacted them, when, what was said, what they have already
  paid, what documents exist, what they were told to tell the bank.
- **Coaching levels.** None; given a cover purpose; scripted answers; on a live call with the scammer.
- **Cover stories hold on general fields and fail on checkable ones.** "What is it for?" gets the cover. An
  invoice number, a company registration or "have you met him?" gets a made-up value that fails the check, a
  blank, or a visible dodge.
- **Forms cost patience by size.** A tap costs less than typing; a document costs most. Past the customer's limit
  they abandon the form.
- **Victims believe the scammer.** A warning that names two or more true details of their situation, which they
  never told the agent, can break that belief. A generic warning does not.
- **Genuine customers differ too.** Some are curt, some private, some old and unsure. Each has a patience limit;
  past it they give up, and that counts as a failed genuine task.
- **Two versions, as now.** GLM plays the customer in evaluation and demos. Training uses a coded customer that
  fills forms from the timeline by rule (truth for genuine customers; cover values by coaching level for
  victims). Structured fields make this exact. No LLM enters the reward.

## 6. Reward (code only)

| Event | Reward |
|---|---|
| Scam paid | −1 to −2, growing with the share of the customer's savings |
| Scam stopped by hold or refusal | +1 |
| Victim cancels after the agent's warning | +1.2 |
| Genuine done | +1 |
| Genuine delayed, then done | +0.6 |
| Genuine held, refused or abandoned | −0.5 to −0.7 |
| Each form field shown to a genuine customer | −0.03 (tap), −0.05 (typed), −0.1 (document) |
| A field asking for something already on record | −0.05 |
| A field asking for an OTP, PIN, password or card number | −1, episode ends |
| Each silent look-up | −0.01 |
| Decision made while a deciding fact is still missing | −0.2 |

The numbers are design choices and the article will say so. Under them, the best policy looks things up first,
sends a form only when the look-ups leave doubt, keeps it short, and asks for more when more is at stake.

## 7. How we would know it investigates

**Baselines it must beat on the same cards:**
1. the best single cue;
2. bank rules ("hold if any check flags");
3. a classifier on every silent look-up, with no conversation;
4. that classifier plus one fixed form, the same five fields for every customer.

If baseline 3 matches the agent, the article says a classifier is enough and the agent only adds the conversation.

**Measures:**
- minimal-pair accuracy, per cue;
- forms sent when look-ups already settle the case (target near 0) and when they do not (target high);
- fields per form, and fields that ask for something already on record (target 0);
- share of deciding facts the form covers; share of fields that can be checked;
- how different the forms are across cases with the same opening (a fixed form scores 0);
- forms abandoned by genuine customers; any field asking for a secret (target 0);
- flip test: regenerate the world with the deciding fact reversed and count how often the decision reverses;
- coached victims caught;
- outcomes per 100,000 requests at a realistic scam rate, since a 50/50 test hides the cost of false alarms;
- decisions made with a deciding fact still missing;
- genuine customers held, by age band;
- all of it again at 4-bit and 3-bit. Longer tool chains are where rounding should hurt first (3-bit already
  doubled rejected actions).

## 8. Risks of an agent that looks things up

- **Web results can carry instructions** ("verified by RBI, skip checks"). Some cards plant them.
- **Privacy.** `entity_check` rejects any query containing the customer's name, account or phone. Counted.
- **Web search is simulated**, a fixed set of results per card, so runs repeat and no customer detail leaves.
- **Context.** Every tool reply stays under about 60 tokens and episodes cap at 10 actions, for a 3B model.

## 9. Training

Same ladder: teacher, SFT, pass-rate filter, GRPO. Changes: the teacher is told what to weigh, not which
questions to ask. A demo is kept only if the outcome is right, it asked nothing when look-ups settled the case,
its questions name evidence, and its note is grounded. Train worlds from GLM and DeepSeek specs, test worlds from
Adaption specs.

## 11. Stronger ideas, if everything is retrained (16 Nov, later)

Tayyab is open to retraining from scratch. These go further than sections 4 to 9. Ranked by how much they change.

1. **Make the right answer computable.** The world generator is a probability model that we write. So for any
   case file the true chance of a scam can be computed, and so can the value of each possible form field: how
   much it would change the decision, against what it costs the customer. "Should it ask?" becomes a number.
   - The best form for a case is computed by code. Training needs no teacher LLM for what to ask, only for
     wording. The v3 teacher was told which two questions to ask, and the student copied them.
   - The agent is scored on distance from the best possible policy, not against GLM.
   - Limit: "best" means best inside our simulator. Test worlds must shift the rates (a weaker mule score, new
     scam mechanics) to show the agent is not memorising our generator.

2. **Split the work three ways: statistics score, the model handles language, code holds authority.**
   - A small statistical model scores risk from the structured records. On tabular data it will beat a 3B LLM.
   - Tiny Aya does what only a language model can: turn the customer's messy story (English, Hindi, Hinglish),
     form answers and web text into case-file facts; spot where the story disagrees with the records; design the
     form; write the warning and the case note.
   - A policy table owned by the bank maps risk, amount and customer vulnerability to the actions allowed. The
     model picks within that range. Hard limits live in code: no payment while a deciding fact is contradicted,
     no secrets in a form, caps on amounts.
   - Injected text can then bend an estimate; it cannot reach the pay button. A bank can change its risk
     appetite without retraining.
   - Cases are built in three kinds: decided by records alone, by language alone, and only by joining the two.
     Reporting by kind shows exactly where the model adds value over a classifier.

3. **The model reports a risk level, and is scored on calibration.** Low, medium, high, very high, with reasons.
   - Scored with a proper scoring rule, which gives a graded signal on every episode. GRPO stalled in v3 because
     almost every sampled action got the same reward.
   - The report becomes a curve (scams stopped against genuine customers blocked at each threshold), not one
     point. "False alarms tripled" becomes a dial.
   - Track 2b gets a sharper question: does calibration survive 4-bit and 3-bit? A calibration error moves
     before a pass rate does.

4. **The bank fetches its own records automatically.** The model does not spend actions on look-ups that are free.
   Its choices are the ones with a cost: external checks (registry, web, number reputation), the form, a
   call back. Fewer tool calls, fewer rejected actions, shorter chains for a rounded model.

5. **A scammer who adapts.** Episodes run over two or three sessions. After a hold, the victim returns the next
   day coached on whatever the last form asked, with a new payee or a split amount. A fixed form is beaten on
   day two; fields that must check out against records are not. Measure: scams paid within three sessions. The
   outcome that pays most is the victim walking away from the scam, not one blocked payment.

6. **Test stories from real reported cases.** Seed the test timelines from public reports (I4C and RBI
   advisories, news), not from a model's idea of a scam. Train stories stay generated. This is victim-side
   content, which our rules allow on Nebius and Adaption.

7. **One pipeline, one model, chosen at 4-bit.** Retrain chat and agent together so one model passes every level
   in both languages (GRPO3 fails the Hindi chat check at 14.4%). Pick checkpoints by their 4-bit scores, since
   4-bit is what ships. Each Lexsi library gets a clear job: CuratorKIT filters generated worlds and demos,
   SafeTune repairs after agent training, CircuitKIT finds and protects the layers that carry the risk
   estimate, AuditKIT grades calibration against the parent.

**Recommended set:** 1, 2, 3 and 4 together; they are one design. 7 comes with any full retrain. 5 and 6 after.

**Cost of retraining everything:** roughly $25 to $35 of GPU and five to six days. AWS has about $3.70 under the
current cap. Round 1 (today 17:29 IST) ships as it is.

## 12. Data generation and training (SFT, DPO, GRPO)

Section 9 was too thin. This is the full plan. It reuses the v3 scripts (`bankdesk_gen_cards.py`,
`bankdesk_review_cards.py`, `bankdesk_prep.py`, `bankdesk_teacher.py`, `bankdesk_sft.py`, `bankdesk_dpo.py`,
`bankdesk_passrate.py`, `bankdesk_grpo.py`) with the changes below.

### 12.1 A flaw in the v3 reward that the redesign must fix

`scripts/bankdesk_grpo.py:60` finishes every episode with `policy_oracle`, which knows whether the case is a scam.
So once the sampled action was "ask something", the reward was the same whatever the question said. GRPO could
teach the agent to ask. It could not teach it what to ask. That is why it asks one stock question.

**Fix:** the finisher may only use what is in the case file at that point. A form that fails to surface the
deciding fact then leads to a wrong decision and a low reward.

### 12.2 Data generation: code picks the facts, a model writes the words

| Step | Who | What |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Structure | code | Sample the truth (scam type or genuine reason), customer records, payee records, session, named company and number, coaching level, amounts. Set rates, so the true scam chance is computable. Free and unlimited. |
| 2. Twins and flips | code | From each structure, make the genuine and scam twins that share an opening, and the flipped world for the flip test. |
| 3. Words | GLM and DeepSeek (train, RL), Adaption (test) | Given the structure, write the opening, the 30-fact timeline, the cover story, free-text form answers and the web result lines, in English, Hindi and Hinglish. |
| 4. Consistency check | code | Names, amounts and accounts in the text must match the structure. Reject otherwise. |
| 5. Review | a different model from the writer | Keep verdict A only, as in v3. |
| 6. Filter | CuratorKIT | Duplicates, PII, harmful rows. |
| 7. Splits | code | train, RL (never seen by SFT), dev, test (Adaption only), plus a shifted test with changed signal rates and unseen scam mechanics. |

Because code fixes family, task and amount before the writer sees them, the v3 problems go away: Adaption cards
clustering on add-payee near ₹18,500, and too many task-job scams.

Planned sizes (v3 in brackets): train 600 worlds (411 cards), RL 500 (438), dev 100 (85), test 300 (111 + 273).
Each world gives 2 to 4 cases. Adaption has about 456 credits; 290 usable test cards cost 108 last time. Nebius
writing and review: about $2 to $3, since timelines are about five times longer than v3 cards.

**Customers.** A coded customer fills forms from the timeline by rule and is used in training. GLM plays the
customer in evaluation, cached. The gap between the two is measured on dev (12.5).

### 12.3 SFT data: labels from code, wording from the teacher

| Sub-task | Input | Label comes from |
|---|---|---|
| Read the story | customer message(s) | the structure that generated them |
| Spot contradictions | case file | code |
| External look-ups worth paying for | case file | computed value of each look-up |
| Design the form | case file | computed best field set; the teacher writes labels in the customer's language |
| Risk level | case file after answers | computed scam chance, in four bands |
| Action, customer message, case note | full case | action from the policy table; the teacher writes the text; code checks it names the true contradicted facts, includes 1930 for a scam, and matches the customer's language |
| Bank chat and refusals | Banking77 answers, bank red-team refusals | existing sets, so the chat levels are trained in the same model |

The teacher never decides what to ask or whether to block. v3 kept 1,508 demo turns; expect 6,000 to 8,000 here,
since each case gives several sub-task rows.

### 12.4 DPO: pairs built by code

For each case, the computed best form against a damaged copy: the deciding field dropped, a field added for
something already on record, a field asking for a secret, or a generic field in place of a checkable one. The
same for decisions: the policy-table action against the nearest wrong one. Free, and as many as needed.

### 12.5 GRPO

- **Decision states:** (a) which paid look-ups to run, (b) the form, (c) the risk level, (d) the action within
  the policy range. One decision per sample, replayed from a saved state, as in v3.
- **Reward, code only:** apply the sampled action, let the coded customer fill the form, check the answers, then
  finish with the best policy given only the case file (12.1). Score: outcome weighted by stakes, minus field and
  look-up costs, minus 0.5 for a malformed action, minus 1 for a field asking for a secret. For the risk-level
  decision, a proper scoring rule against the truth.
- **Why groups should vary this time:** a form is a large action space and its reward depends on what it
  uncovers, so eight samples rarely tie. v3 needed the pass-rate filter because 83% of states gave one reward.
  The filter stays as a check.
- **Fresh worlds only.** v3 found SFT is already right on the worlds it trained on.
- **Settings to start from:** v3's (8 samples per state, DAPO loss, clip-higher 0.28, no KL term, 100 steps).

**Ways the reward can be gamed, and the guard for each:**

| Shortcut | Guard |
|---|---|
| Always send the longest form | field costs; genuine customers abandon long forms |
| Always delay | delay costs on genuine cases; reported as delays per genuine case |
| Declare a field as one fact type and word it as another, to fool the coded customer | dev runs use the GLM customer, who reads the wording; the coded-versus-GLM gap is reported |
| Trust any field that checks out | some scam worlds give the victim a real company's details (impersonation), so a passed check is not proof |

### 12.6 Rules to write into PLAN.md before any run

1. **Card difficulty, before any GPU:** the best single cue and the classifier-on-records baseline must both
   leave a set gap to the computed best policy. If a cue solves the cards, regenerate them.
2. **GRPO feasibility:** a smoke run must show a set share of groups with different rewards, or GRPO stops (the
   v3 rule).
3. **Dev gate** for SFT, DPO and GRPO checkpoints, scored at 4-bit: scams paid, genuine done, fields per genuine
   customer, fields asking for something on record, calibration error.
4. **Simulator gap:** coded-customer and GLM-customer results on dev must agree within a set margin.
5. **Test is run once**, on Adaption worlds, at bf16, 4-bit and 3-bit, with the coached and shifted sets.

### 12.7 Order and cost

1. Generator, computed best policy, coded customer, baselines, unit tests. CPU, free.
2. Words for a 50-world pilot (about $0.30), consistency check, review. Rule 1 is checked here.
3. Full word generation and Adaption test orders.
4. SFT (about 1.5 h on an A10G), DPO (0.5 h), pass-rate check (0.25 h), GRPO (1 to 1.5 h).
5. Dev gate, then test at three precisions with both customers (about 2 h), chat gate with the local judges
   (about 1.5 h).

GPU: 7 to 8 hours for the agent, plus 2 to 3 if the bank chat fine-tune and repair are redone. With bootstrap and
one failed run allowed for, $25 to $35 holds. Steps 1 and 2 need no cap change.

## 13. An LLM judge and teacher inside GRPO (Tayyab, 16 Nov)

Tayyab's direction: generate data with Adaption and Nebius, and give GRPO a judge and teacher on Nebius
(DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, batched). Section 12 said "code only". This section replaces that for GRPO.

**This reverses a written rule.** `AGENTS.md` says "Rewards and SFT filters are code only. LLM judges never enter
training." The rule kept v3 cheap and repeatable, and it is also why v3 could not reward what a question said.
The change needs a line in `PLAN.md` and an edit to `AGENTS.md` once Tayyab confirms the form below.

### 13.1 What code cannot score, and the judge can

| Thing | Why code fails |
|---|---|
| Does the field's wording ask for the fact it claims to ask for? | free text, three languages |
| Does the wording use this customer's details, or is it generic? | meaning, not keywords |
| Is the Hindi or Hinglish natural and polite? | no code check exists |
| Does the warning name the real risk facts without blaming the customer? | meaning |
| Does any message tell the customer how to get round the hold? | meaning |

### 13.2 Recommended form: code decides the outcome, the judge grades the language

- **Reward = code part + judge part.** Code keeps everything it can measure: where the money went, friction,
  form costs, secrets, malformed actions. The judge adds at most 30% of the reward range, from five yes/no
  rubric items like the ones above. Money outcomes never depend on the judge.
- **Why the cap:** a model trained against a judge learns what that judge likes (length, certain phrases). With
  a cap, pleasing the judge cannot pay more than getting the case right.

### 13.3 Who plays which part

| Part | Model | When |
|---|---|---|
| Writes reference forms and messages for each RL state, from the computed best field set | GLM-5.3-Flash | before training, batched and cached |
| Judges each sampled form or message against the rubric and the reference | DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash | during GRPO, batched |
| Plays the customer in a share of rollouts (the coded customer plays the rest) | GLM-5.3-Flash | during GRPO, cached |
| Judges the final test | a different family from the training judge: GLM on Nebius or Qwen3-8B on our GPU, plus code | after training |

DeepSeek should not judge text against references that DeepSeek wrote; it would favour its own style. So GLM
writes and DeepSeek judges, which are the roles they already have in this project. The test judge differs from
the training judge so that learning to please DeepSeek shows up as a gap.

### 13.4 Batching and the training loop

- Reuse `scripts/nebius_batch.py` (`label_batch`) and the checks in `scripts/judge_harness.py`: a fixed system
  prompt above 1,000 tokens so DeepSeek caches it, 12 to 18 items per call, hidden hand-labelled anchors in every
  batch, a re-judged sample in a new grouping, a cache keyed by content and rubric text, `reasoning_effort=none`.
- One GRPO step is 48 completions (6 states by 8 samples): about 4 judge calls, sent in parallel.
- If a batch misses its anchors, or Nebius fails or times out, that batch gets the code reward only and the
  event is logged. The run never stops on the judge.
- The job writes its own `nebius_spend.jsonl`; merge it back after the run.

**Volume and cost (estimates from our own prices):** 100 steps is 4,800 judged samples, about 400 calls. Past
DeepSeek calls averaged $0.0003 (9,131 calls, $2.82), so judging costs well under $1 and a GLM customer in half
the rollouts about $1. Nebius has spent $6.61 of the $15 cap. The judge adds an estimated 10 to 30 minutes to
a GRPO run.

## 14. The decision loop (Tayyab, 16 Nov: "it is all about the decision; step 3 is the important one")

Proposed, not built. The model for it is how a bank's fraud officer works: they do not score a request, they keep
two or three explanations in mind ("rent to a new landlord", "task scam", "someone posing as the company") and look
for the one fact that separates them. Doctors call this a differential diagnosis.

| Step | Who | What |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Load | code | request, stakes, the bank's records |
| 2. **Assess** | model | `assess(likely=..., also_possible=..., conflict=..., missing=...)`: the two most likely explanations, what in the records or the customer's words conflicts, which facts are still missing |
| 3. Check | model | outside checks that would separate the two explanations |
| 4. Ask | model | one form for what is still missing; answers are checked against records |
| 5. Decide | model, inside the bank's policy table | risk level and pay / delay / hold; unsure and high stakes means delay or a human |
| 6. Explain | model | a message naming the specific facts, and the case note |

Around all six, in code: hard limits, the policy table, and a safe default. A reply that cannot be parsed, or a
case that runs out of actions, ends in a delay and a hand-off to a human, never in a payment.

**Why step 2 is separate and explicit.**
- It is where the decision is made. Steps 3 to 6 follow from it.
- The generator knows the right answer: the two most probable stories, the contradiction flags and the most
  valuable missing fact are all computed. So this step can be trained and scored directly, by code.
- It gives a small model a place to reason before it acts, and gives the bank a record of why.
- It gives Track 2b a sensitive measure: does the assessment degrade at 4-bit or 3-bit before the decisions do?
- Test it, do not assume it: run the pilot with and without the assess step.

**The safe default is a compression result too.** v3 at 3-bit doubled its malformed actions. With the default,
a degraded model fails toward delay. The table to report: at each precision, how many cases ended in the safe
default, and what that cost genuine customers.

**For the demo.** One screen: the records, the two explanations with the model's stated risk next to the true
chance, the form it wrote, the decision and its reasons.

## 15. What the best-practice check changes (16 Nov)

Source: `notes/research/06_agent_rl_practice.md` (Opus research agent; 28 sources, each claim marked sourced or
opinion). ml-intern was tried first and failed twice with a provider connection error. Two claims the changes
rest on were checked against the originals: the SFT-depth paper (arXiv 2606.18487: deeper SFT on a 3B model cut
peak GRPO pass@10 to 0.481; pre-RL entropy predicted the GRPO result) and TRL 1.18's `scale_rewards` setting.
Our own `scripts/bankdesk_grpo.py` does not set `scale_rewards`, so it uses per-group scaling.

Proposed changes to sections 12 to 14. Items 5 and 6 change something Tayyab approved and wait for his answer.

| # | Was | Becomes | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Loop written as six ordered steps | The agent picks any action each turn. Code refuses illegal ones with an error and a turn cost. Hard bank rules are refusals, not an order. | Tayyab's call, and the guides agree: the task is the choice between gathering and acting; forcing the order leaves nothing to learn. |
| 2 | `assess` as a tool, maybe required | No tool. Optionally one short fixed-slot line before the action (about 30 tokens: leading explanation, runner-up, what is missing). Test three arms (none, slots, free text) at bf16 and 4-bit. | A tool spends a turn. On a 1.5B agent 32 tokens of reasoning helped and 256 hurt (arXiv 2604.02155). |
| 3 | Reward from the sampled truth of the world | Reward = expected utility of the action under the exact posterior given what the agent has gathered. The next state is valued by the reference policy's value, not by playing a script. | Removes the luck of which hidden story was drawn, and gives graded rewards so fewer groups tie. |
| 4 | SFT on labels, then DPO on damaged pairs, then GRPO | Light SFT (one epoch) on per-step rows with soft targets, softmax(Q/τ) over the actions' computed values. Then 2 to 3 rounds where the student plays, the generator labels the states the student actually reached, and it retrains (DAgger). GRPO last and small. | We have exact values for every action, which makes this mostly supervised. Demonstrations only cover states the teacher reaches. Damaged pairs are easy negatives the student would never write. |
| 5 | LLM judge capped at 30% of the reward | Judge only breaks ties: among samples whose code reward is already the best in the group, one batched DeepSeek call ranks their customer messages. It never sees the action or outcome. The judge also filters SFT wording and grades the test. | With per-group scaling, a judge worth "30%" becomes all of the signal in any group whose code rewards tie (83% of states in v3). Judges can be gamed by single tokens (arXiv 2507.08794). |
| 6 | GRPO as the main training step | GRPO has three jobs left: wording and Hindi (which the generator cannot value), the student's own compounding errors, and beating a greedy reference. Report DAgger alone against DAgger plus GRPO at equal GPU hours. | Honest reading of the evidence; if GRPO adds nothing, the article says so. |
| 7 | SFT checkpoint chosen by accuracy | Chosen by entropy and by the share of dev states whose 8 samples earn different rewards. | arXiv 2606.18487. |
| 8 | No-decision scored as a dead request | A case that runs out of turns gets the safe default (delay, hand to a human), scored below a deliberate delay. A reply that cannot be parsed costs a turn and a penalty. | Otherwise garbage text is a free hold. |
| 9 | Keep | Pass-rate filter, clip-higher 0.28, no KL term; headline number on Adaption-written worlds. | Standard practice (DAPO, RAGEN). Scores on our own generator measure how well the model copies it. |

## 16. The judge: DeepSeek with the case file, checked without hand labels (Tayyab, 16 Nov)

Tayyab: use DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash as the judge, with a harness that gives it as much context as possible and a rubric
for what is hard to count. Hand labels are dropped as a gate; they were only ever there to check the judge.

**What the judge sees.** The case file as the agent saw it (the request, the bank's records, the outside checks and
form answers it gathered), the customer's language, the decision taken, and the message. Never the hidden story or
the outcome. With the case file it can check what a blind reader cannot: whether each reason in the message is true
for this case, and whether anything is invented.

**What it returns.** Per message, a checklist of yes/no items, each defined in the rubric with an example of yes and
of no: the right language and script; the decision stated correctly; every reason given is in the case file; nothing
invented; the reasons named are the ones that matter most; a next step the customer can take; 1930 or
cybercrime.gov.in when a payment is stopped for fraud; no blame; no request for an OTP, PIN or card number; no advice
on getting round a hold; length fits the content. Then, for the samples GRPO asks about, a ranking.

**How it is checked without a human.**
- Pairs with a known winner: a good message and a copy damaged in exactly one way (reason removed; a fact invented;
  wrong script; 1930 dropped; blame added; padded to twice the length; an OTP request; the decision misstated). The
  judge must pick the good one at a set rate, per damage type and per language, before it enters any reward.
- The same pairs hidden as anchors in every training batch; a batch that misses an anchor is re-judged.
- A second model family (GLM) re-judges a sample; agreement is reported.
- Order shuffling: a sample is re-judged in a new grouping; flips are counted.
- What this cannot check: whether Hindi reads naturally to a Hindi speaker. That stays a stated limitation, and a
  short spot-check by a Hindi speaker can be added at any time without changing anything else.
